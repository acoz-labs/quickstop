# Workflow Artifacts

Durable markdown a workflow produces for a human to read: design notes, investigation write-ups, decision records, and scratch docs. Write it to be digested while retaining what readers need to understand and evaluate it. The artifact holds the conclusions and their supporting evidence, not a transcript of the conversation. Later readers get depth from accessible citations and supporting sections without needing the original session.

## The contract

1. **Lead with what matters.** Every artifact opens with the verdict, decision, or ask, stated plainly and completely enough to act on after reading nothing else. A short paragraph is fine; a teaser line is not. Then add a short **Needs my attention** list for open questions, decisions, and scope-widening items. The gist lands in 30 seconds.
2. **Structure for scanning.** Use tables for anything with repeating shape. Put one idea in each bullet. Use short sections under meaningful headings. Keep paragraphs focused; line count depends on the display and is not a limit on necessary context.
3. **Use plain speech.** Apply the output-style rules: spoken prose, no filler, no evasive hedging, preserve factual uncertainty, and no restating what a diagram or table already shows.
4. **Keep understanding visible and supporting evidence accessible.** Conclusions, decisions, meaningful uncertainty, consequences and context needed to understand or evaluate a claim stay visible, using however many words clarity needs. Keep short evidence nearby. Lengthy optional proof chains, quotations and verification traces can follow in plain Markdown sections, appendices or links. This is the default for ordinary documents and unknown renderers; do not fold every evidence section automatically. GitHub comments and PR descriptions may use `<details>` for lengthy optional supporting evidence. Other destinations may use documented native collapsible blocks when useful and supported. Do not invent syntax or assume Markdown supports raw HTML. A collapsed block may supply further verification, but must not hide what a reader needs to assess the claim or make a decision.
5. **Use visuals where structure exists.** Use diagrams for flows, sequences, and relationships. A table beats a bulleted narrative; a diagram beats a table when the shape is a graph.

   **Node labels are prose and follow the same rules.** A box reading `GalleryPreview.arrangeTiles` is a diagram nobody can read without the codebase open, and a diagram is usually the first thing examined. Label nodes with what the step does in words ("Group pictures by collection, then arrange each group") and put the symbol underneath or in the walkthrough. The exception is where the identifier is the subject: database tables in an entity-relationship diagram are named what they are named, and renaming them for readability would be misleading.
6. **Write conclusions, not the journey.** Never transcribe the discussion into the artifact. Keep citations out of the sentence carrying the meaning: the prose makes its point in plain words, while `file:line`, ticket ids, and quoted sources sit in a trailing parenthetical or a supporting evidence section using the destination-appropriate format above. An identifier is never the subject or verb of a sentence someone has to read.
7. **Write for someone who was not there.** Every part of the body survives the context-independence test in the output style: no term coined this session is used as shared vocabulary, no cross-document pointer stands in for what it says (quote the criterion; do not write “AC 3”), and no sentence needs an open file to make sense. Anything asking for a decision follows `questions.md` and may use the bounded, optional check in `cold-reader.md`.
8. **Check the reading burden.** A short opening helps readers find the point; there is no fixed screen limit. Remove repetition and place optional depth where it is easy to find. Keep the context and evidence needed to understand, evaluate or act on the document, even when that requires a longer body. Never hide or delete necessary material just to meet a size target.
