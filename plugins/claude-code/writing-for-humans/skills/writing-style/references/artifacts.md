# Workflow Artifacts

Durable markdown a workflow produces for a human to read: design notes, investigation write-ups, decision records, and scratch docs. Write it to be digested, not for completeness. The conversation holds the journey; the artifact holds the conclusions. Later stages and subagents still get their depth from citations and collapsed sections, never from prose sprawl in the body.

## The contract

1. **Lead with what matters.** Every artifact opens with the verdict, decision, or ask, stated plainly and completely enough to act on after reading nothing else. A short paragraph is fine; a teaser line is not. Then add a short **Needs my attention** list for open questions, decisions, and scope-widening items. The gist lands in 30 seconds.
2. **Structure for scanning.** Use tables for anything with repeating shape. Put one idea in each bullet. Use short sections under meaningful headings. Keep paragraphs to about 4 lines.
3. **Use plain speech.** Apply the output-style rules: spoken prose, no filler, no evasive hedging, preserve factual uncertainty, and no restating what a diagram or table already shows.
4. **The body carries understanding; `<details>` carries verification.** Everything needed to understand and evaluate the artifact stays in the body, at summary altitude, using however many words clarity needs. Collapse proof chains, quoted evidence, per-item verification traces, and the mechanics of how a fact was established. Expanding a block should answer “How do we know?”, never “What does this mean?” If someone must expand it to understand, it is over-collapsed.
5. **Use visuals where structure exists.** Use diagrams for flows, sequences, and relationships. A table beats a bulleted narrative; a diagram beats a table when the shape is a graph.

   **Node labels are prose and follow the same rules.** A box reading `GalleryPreview.arrangeTiles` is a diagram nobody can read without the codebase open, and a diagram is usually the first thing examined. Label nodes with what the step does in words ("Group pictures by collection, then arrange each group") and put the symbol underneath or in the walkthrough. The exception is where the identifier is the subject: database tables in an entity-relationship diagram are named what they are named, and renaming them for readability would be misleading.
6. **Write conclusions, not the journey.** Never transcribe the discussion into the artifact. Keep citations out of the sentence carrying the meaning: the prose makes its point in plain words, while `file:line`, ticket ids, and quoted sources sit in a trailing parenthetical or a `<details>` block. An identifier is never the subject or verb of a sentence someone has to read.
7. **Write for someone who was not there.** Every part of the body survives the context-independence test in the output style: no term coined this session is used as shared vocabulary, no cross-document pointer stands in for what it says (quote the criterion; do not write “AC 3”), and no sentence needs an open file to make sense. Anything asking for a decision follows `questions.md` and may use the bounded, optional check in `cold-reader.md`.
8. **Check the size before writing.** If the uncollapsed body would not fit on about 2 screens, it carries too much implementation or verification detail. Collapse it or cut it. A section that does not change what the reader does next does not belong in the body.
